The question: Which customer segments actually drive chip category sales? Perform A/B testing to examine the effect of a new store layout on store performance.

Approach: Cleaned and merged transaction and customer datasets covering 246K+ records, then segmented customers to find that a small set of segments collectively, accounted for roughly 25% of total chip sales. I ran affinity analysis to see which products each segment over-indexed on. Then for next task, I designed a trial-vs-control store matching framework using correlation and magnitude distance so any sales lift could be attributed to the trial, not to store-to-store noise.

Outcome: Top sales segments are Budget Older Families, Mainstream Young Singles/Couples, and Mainstream Retirees, suggested segment-specific promotional campaigns and category planning as they contribute the highest sales and a statistically defensible A/B test design that could support a real store-layout rollout.

The question: Across 2 campaign types (A = conversational, B = promotional) and 3 channels (Email, Instagram, Web Banner), where should marketing spend go if the top priority is new customer growth?

Approach: Analyzed 500+ customer interactions and calculated 12 key performance metrics including conversion rate, revenue, efficiency, weighted scores, and supporting breakdowns. These metrics were compared across campaign–channel combinations to identify which drivers most strongly influenced new customer outcomes. To make the comparison fair across very different channel scales, I built a weighted ranking model rather than relying on raw revenue alone.

Outcome: Campaign A + Email came out ~54% higher on revenue potential than Campaign B + Email, and the ranking model flagged Email specifically as the strongest driver of new customer acquisition — a concrete case for reallocating budget.

The question: what do 3,900+ purchase records across 18 features actually say about who buys, what they buy, and whether they'll stay subscribed?

Approach: Cleaned and prepared the dataset using Python (Pandas) — handling missing values, standardizing columns, and creating age-group segments — then loaded it into MySQL for SQL-based business analysis covering revenue by gender/age, subscriber vs non-subscriber spending, discount usage, and repeat buyer behaviour. Findings were then visualized in a Power BI dashboard covering Executive Summary, Customer Behaviour, Product Performance, and Operational Insights.

Outcome: Subscribers generated higher and more consistent revenue than non-subscribers, and certain age groups contributed disproportionately to total sales. Repeat customers proved to be a major growth driver, while heavy reliance on discounts was found to boost volume but risk profit margins. Top-rated products emerged as strong candidates for targeted promotion, and shipping preferences showed a clear link to customer spending patterns — together pointing toward stronger retention programs, smarter discount strategies, and demographic-focused marketing as key next steps.

The question: How can sales, customer, product, and store data from a multinational grocery chain (Canada, Mexico, US) be analyzed to track performance, profitability, and customer behavior for data-backed decisions?

Approach: Cleaned data in Power Query; built relational data model in Power BI, created DAX measures for total revenue, total profit, profit margin percent, total transactions, return rate percent, avg revenue per customer; finally built 4 dashboards features — topline performance, product analysis, customer analysis, store performance.

Outcome: US led in both revenue and transaction volume across all regions, making it the primary driver of overall performance. A small set of top products accounted for the majority of sales, while certain product categories showed notably higher return rates, pointing to potential quality or expectation issues. Store performance varied significantly by region, reflecting differences in local demand and operations, and clear seasonal peaks emerged in the data — offering useful signals for future inventory and promotional planning.

Good analysis isn't enough if the insight gets lost in the explanation.
One framework that has changed the way I communicate my work is the "Pyramid Principle" — starting with the answer, then organizing the reasoning and evidence underneath it. As an engineer, I naturally tend to explain how I solved something. The Pyramid Principle has pushed me to think more about what matters first: What is the answer? Why does it matter? And what evidence supports it? I'm still practicing it, but it has changed the way I approach dashboards, presentations, and even everyday communication.
The goal isn't to make analysis more complicated, it's to make the thinking easier to follow.
